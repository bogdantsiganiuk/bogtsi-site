A first warm-up, without chasing high notes. Follow the exercise order of Voxwarm's free five-minute Singer routine: guided deep-belly breathing, a five-note scale, a triad, an octave siren, a lip-trill five-note pattern, and a hummed triad. This printable guide explains each step without requiring the app, a recording, or an internet connection.

Before you start: Choose a quiet moment and a comfortable, light volume. Stay within notes that feel easy today, not your highest or lowest notes. If your voice already feels painful, strained, or hoarse, skip the singing and rest instead.

Set up a small, comfortable practice

  • The app labels this Singer routine five minutes. This sheet follows its released order, not a second-by-second timetable. Aim for about five minutes only if comfortable; pauses, repetitions, and skipped steps can make your paper practice shorter or longer. There is no requirement to finish every exercise.
  • A piano, another simple instrument, or a willing helper can demonstrate the note patterns below. Listen to the whole phrase, let the sound finish, then sing it back. No instrument or helper? Use a familiar starting pitch and the shapes below only if you know them; skip any shape you cannot comfortably find rather than forcing or guessing through it.
  • Choose a starting note that leaves room for the whole phrase inside your comfortable range. Lower the starting pitch or skip the exercise if its top note feels like a reach. An octave is a wider span than the scale and triad; it is optional, not a range test.

The released Singer routine, in order

Keep repetitions brief and leave silence for an ordinary breath between phrases. The numbers below give the order, not minutes assigned to each exercise. Whenever a reference sound is available, listen first and answer only after it stops.

  1. Guided deep-belly breathing

    Sit or stand comfortably and let your shoulders settle. The app's opening breath uses a four-second inhale, a two-second hold, and a six-second exhale, with the cue “Breathe low into your belly.” On paper, follow that count only if it feels easy: do not take a maximum breath or force a hold. If the pacing is uncomfortable or you feel lightheaded, stop the exercise and return to ordinary breathing.

  2. Five-note scale · “ah”

    Sing the first five notes of a major scale up and back down: 1–2–3–4–5–4–3–2–1. For an instrument reference, the shape starting on C is C–D–E–F–G–F–E–D–C; choose a different starting pitch if that is more comfortable. Sing a light “ah” on each note, keeping your jaw relaxed.

  3. Triad · “ah”

    A triad uses the first, third, and fifth notes of that same scale: 1–3–5–3–1. Starting on C, that is C–E–G–E–C. Listen, let the reference stop, then sing “ah” through the shape with smooth, light changes between notes rather than punching each leap.

  4. Octave siren · “oo”

    An octave reaches from one note to the next note with the same name above it, such as C to the next C. Only if that whole span is easy for you, glide gently upward on “oo” from the lower note to the upper one, without separate steps in between. Do not search for your highest note. Skip this block if an octave does not feel comfortable today.

  5. Lip-trill five-note pattern · “brr”

    Return to the five-note shape: 1–2–3–4–5–4–3–2–1. Let your lips vibrate loosely on “brr” as you follow it, with unforced airflow. If a lip trill does not happen easily, leave this block out rather than blowing harder or tightening your jaw.

  6. Hum triad · “hmm”

    Finish the ordered sequence with the same 1–3–5–3–1 triad, this time humming gently with your lips closed. Keep it light and comfortable. Afterward, stop singing and rest; you do not need to add a higher note or another repetition.

Comfort comes before the clock

Stop and rest if you feel pain, strain, or hoarseness. Do not sing through symptoms to finish a phrase, reach five minutes, or complete the sequence. Ask a qualified professional about persistent vocal symptoms.

This guide describes the released routine's exercise order in plain language. It is practice guidance, not a clinically validated routine, medical care, or a promise to extend your range. It cannot assess your voice or replace individual guidance. Skipping a block or stopping early is always an option.
